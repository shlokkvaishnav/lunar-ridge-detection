Lunar ridge detection

A lunar wrinkle ridge detection system, built and tested with three approaches: classical phase-symmetry morphology, Hessian-based ridge filtering, and deep learning. All three run on the same elevation tiles, to find out which ridges each method finds, which it misses, and why.

Three methods, one benchmark

  1. Method A: phase symmetry. Classical morphology. It filters the shape of the fragments it finds before it links gaps. On real tiles this roughly tripled recall compared with the first approach, which thresholded by percentile and then closed gaps.
  2. Method B: Hessian ridge filter. The Frangi filter, with the same post-processing as method A, so the comparison isolates the filter itself.
  3. Method C: deep learning. A small two-branch convolutional network on the elevation map and its aspect, inspired by DBR-Net. It trained on a Kaggle GPU, on 114 of the 120 tiles. The 6 benchmark tiles were held out. The labels are weak: buffers drawn around ridge polylines, not hand-labeled masks.

The data is a pilot set of 120 tiles taken from the remote LROC WAC and GLD100 mosaics, with no full download. The tiles are spread across the Thompson et al. 2017 ridge catalog. The benchmark is 6 real tiles with 57 ridge vertices. A vertex counts as found when the predicted mask covers its exact pixel.

Results

Which ridges each method finds

Of the 21 vertices that both classical methods miss, the deep-learning model finds 14. A random mask with the same coverage on each tile would find about 6 of them (p = 0.0002, if the vertices are treated as independent, which is optimistic).

What these results do not show

  • This holds on one benchmark: 6 tiles, 57 vertices and two checkpoints. It does not show that method C has no blind spots in general.
  • The deep-learning result is not yet a settled win. After adjusting for coverage, the epoch-7 checkpoint is weaker than the first run's final one (1.96 times against 2.26 times better than a random mask).
  • The two checkpoints do not agree on which vertices they find. A statement about one vertex holds for one checkpoint, or where both agree.
  • Part of the classical methods' blind spot comes from the exact-pixel scoring. With a tolerance of 2 pixels, the vertices that both methods miss shrink from 21 to 12. Method C was not scored with a tolerance.
  • The labels are weak, and the network is smaller than DBR-Net. That is a real gap in supervision quality.
